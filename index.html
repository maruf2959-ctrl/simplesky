The honest twist

The mount and focal length matter more than the camera

For all of M42 and any of M31, a used DSLR and lens on a £435 Star Adventurer GTi beats any camera on the 127 SLT. Here's why.

What your telescope and mount really allow

9×more exposure needed at f/12 than at f/4 for the same signal per pixel ((12/4)²)
0.4–0.6″per pixel at 1,500 mm. That's finer than UK seeing (2–3″) supports, so tracking errors show instantly
2–8 srealistic sub length on the SLT at 1,500 mm. 10 s works sometimes, near the horizon in the east or west
200–2,000frames to stack, from bright M42 to faint galaxies

1. Focal length and field of view

At 1,500 mm, an APS-C DSLR sees about 51′ × 34′. M42 with its wings spans about 65′ × 60′, so a DSLR gets the bright core and most of the nebula but crops the outer loops. An ASI585MC sees 26′ × 14′: the Trapezium and the inner core only, unless you use the 0.5× reducer. M31 is about 3° across, six times wider than a DSLR's field here. On the 127 SLT you can only photograph its core. The small galaxies (M51 at 11′ × 7′, M81 at 27′ × 14′) frame nicely, but they are faint at f/12.

2. f/12 brightness

Surface brightness depends on focal ratio. At f/12 each pixel gets about 5.8× less light than at f/5 and 9× less than at f/4. Since you're also limited to short subs, each frame is mostly noise for anything fainter than M42's core. That's why read noise matters more for you than cooling or megapixels. A ZWO camera at ≈0.8 e⁻ is barely penalised for splitting an hour into 600 × 6 s, while a 600D at ≈3 e⁻ needs roughly twice the total time for a similar result on faint targets.

3. Field rotation (alt-az)

An alt-az mount keeps the target centred, but the frame slowly rotates around it. The rate is ω = 15.04″/s × cos(latitude) × cos(azimuth) ÷ cos(altitude). From Worthing (50.8°N), M42 at its highest (≈34°, due south) rotates about 11″ per second in angle terms. The longest sub before a corner star smears by one pixel is therefore about 5–6 s on a 600D and 8 s on an ASI585MC. The "Tonight" table above calculates this live. Stacking software (DeepSkyStacker, Siril, SharpCap live stack) de-rotates between frames, and you crop the ragged edges.

4. Tracking accuracy

The SLT mount was designed for visual use. Its plastic gears have backlash and periodic error, and at 0.4–0.6″/px that shows as elongated stars within seconds. Owners report 10 s subs being stackable at high ISO on bright objects, but many frames need rejecting. Plan for 2–6 s subs and a 50–80% keep rate, and use the "best frames" filter in DeepSkyStacker or Siril. Balance the camera carefully (use the dovetail position), approach targets with final GoTo moves upward and to the right to take up backlash, and power the mount from a 12 V supply. The SLT eats AA batteries, and low voltage ruins tracking.

6. Planets: when to use the 2× Barlow

The ideal focal ratio for a colour planetary camera is about 5 × pixel size (µm), give or take for seeing:

  • ASI662MC / ASI585MC (2.9 µm): ideal ≈f/14. Native f/12 is nearly perfect. A 2× Barlow (f/24) only helps on rare steady nights; otherwise it makes a bigger, blurrier, dimmer image. A 1.5× setting (Barlow with a shorter spacer) is the sweet spot if you want more scale. AstroBin users debating 585 vs 662 reach the same conclusion.
  • ASI224MC (3.75 µm): ideal ≈f/19–20, so the 2× Barlow gets close. A Stargazers Lounge thread with a Mak 127 calls it "close to the ideal focal ratio".
  • Canon 600D (4.3 µm, movie crop): ideal ≈f/21, so always use the Barlow.
  • The R6 II you borrowed (6 µm): ideal ≈f/30. That's why Saturn looked small; a dedicated camera at f/12–f/16 will out-resolve it.

Whatever the camera, the 127 mm aperture sets the resolution limit at about 0.9″. Seeing, collimation and cooling the Mak for 45–60 minutes matter more than anything you buy. Shoot 60–90 s video clips for Jupiter (it rotates fast) and up to 3 min for Saturn, then stack the best 10–25% in AutoStakkert, as you already do.

DSLR vs dedicated astro camera

Used DSLR (600D / D5300)

  • Big APS-C field frames M42
  • Works standalone with an intervalometer
  • Doubles as a camera-lens astro camera (M31, Milky Way)
  • £110–£260 used
  • Higher read noise penalises 2–8 s subs
  • Heavy for the SLT arm
  • Planetary video is limited to 640×480 crop on the 600D and isn't usable on the D5300
  • The IR-cut filter blocks most H-alpha

Uncooled ZWO (662 / 585)

  • Read noise is 3–4× lower, ideal for short-sub stacking
  • 100+ fps planetary video, pixel-matched to f/12
  • Light (≈130 g), so the SLT tracks better
  • SharpCap live stack with field de-rotation
  • Small sensors: M42 needs the reducer or a mosaic
  • Laptop needed at the scope
  • Useless for daytime photography

Can one camera do both? Yes, the ASI585MC is that camera for this telescope. Two specialised cameras (an ASI662MC plus a used 600D, about £330) cost less than you'd think and cover M42's full field. Is a cooled camera worth it? Not on this mount. Cooling reduces dark current, which only matters on subs of a minute or more. On 2–8 s subs the uncooled 585 performs essentially identically to the £619 585 Pro.

Exact adapters & total cost

Compatibility note: the 127 SLT's rear port takes the visual back and diagonal. The universally safe imaging path is to remove the star diagonal and push a 1.25″ camera nosepiece into the visual back. This is what 127 SLT owners on Stargazers Lounge use, and the Mak focuses by moving its mirror, so there is plenty of focus travel. Thread-on adapters (Celestron's 4SE/C90 T-adapter, Mak-to-SCT rings) are made for other Maksutovs and are not confirmed to fit the 127 SLT, so don't buy them without checking your rear thread. The Celestron f/6.3 reducer needs SCT threads and isn't compatible.

Strongly recommended for everything

  • Dew shield (≈£20–£30, or a DIY camping-mat tube). A Mak's corrector dews up within an hour on the Sussex coast.
  • Bahtinov mask for 127 mm (≈£15–£25). Focus at 1,500 mm is extremely sensitive.
  • 12 V power for the SLT (≈£30–£60 for a power tank or mains adapter). This gives stable tracking and no flat AAs.
  • Software (all free unless noted): SharpCap (free; Pro is ≈£15/yr for live-stack extras), ASIStudio, N.I.N.A., DeepSkyStacker, Siril, AutoStakkert, RegiStax. BackyardEOS is paid but optional.

Challenging the assumptions

"I want M42's colours and M31": use a camera lens, not the Mak

M42's full extent and M31 want 135–300 mm focal length, not 1,500 mm. A used Canon 600D with an EF-S 55–250 IS STM or EF 70–200 f/4 on a Sky-Watcher Star Adventurer GTi (£435, FLO) can take 30–120 s subs at f/4–f/5.6, which is 5–9× brighter per pixel and roughly 10× longer subs. One hour of that will beat a whole night of 4 s Mak frames on M42, and it actually fits M31. The GTi is an equatorial mount, so there is no field rotation.

Cheapest version: piggyback the DSLR and lens on the 127 SLT itself, which tracks fine at 135 mm for 15–30 s subs. It still has field rotation, but at that scale it's far more forgiving. It costs just a ball head and a bracket.

Mount upgrade vs camera upgrade

For deep sky, yes: a mount upgrade helps more than any camera. But a mount strong enough to guide a 1,500 mm Mak well (EQ5 Pro / HEQ5 class) costs about £800–£1,200 plus guiding gear, which is outside this budget. The GTi carries the 127 Mak (≈3.5 kg with a camera) only marginally and won't guide well at 1,500 mm. Its real job is wide-field with lenses. An equatorial wedge for the SLT fork is a poor buy: the arm isn't stiff enough and polar alignment is awkward.

Smart telescope?

If you mostly want pretty M42, M31 and nebula pictures with minimal fuss, a ZWO Seestar S50 (£539, FLO) (250 mm f/5, with a built-in alt-az and field-rotation handling) out-performs the 127 SLT on large nebulae. It's poor for planets, though, so the Mak and a 662 stay the planetary rig.

Where to put your money

  1. Mostly planets: ASI662MC (£199). This is the clearest upgrade over the R6 II.
  2. Planets plus small deep-sky with one camera: ASI585MC (£399).
  3. M42 and M31 really are the priority: used 600D (£150) + Star Adventurer GTi (£435) + used lens (£80), then add a 662 for planets.
